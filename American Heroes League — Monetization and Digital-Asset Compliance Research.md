# American Heroes League — Monetization and Digital-Asset Compliance Research

## Patreon

Patreon’s Commerce Guidelines prohibit offering financial products, investment schemes, or investment opportunities; they also state that crowdfunding generally may not be offered through Patreon. Patreon’s Community Guidelines say Patreon may not be used to launch cryptocurrencies, sell equity/ownership in companies, or offer investment opportunities. It does permit original creative works, transparent parody/satire, memberships, exclusive content, merchandise, and one-time purchases subject to its policies. [1] [2]

**Operational conclusion:** Patreon can host paid memberships and defined creative benefits, but it should not be used to sell an NFT/crypto launch, crowdfund a token, solicit capital, offer revenue share, or frame membership as an investment.

Patreon’s creator launch guidance recommends beginning with a simple offering, preparing immediate value before inviting members, using paid memberships or one-time purchases, and expanding benefits only after capacity is proven. Its annual-membership page says annual plans have eligibility conditions, including page age and earnings history; the criteria can be subject to restrictions. [3] [4]

## Reward-Based Crowdfunding Alternative

Kickstarter’s rules require honest presentation, rights-cleared materials, transparent AI use, and a plan to create something new. Its current rules list cryptocurrencies and NFTs as prohibited items/applications. Kickstarter also says its platform cannot be used to offer financial returns, equity, or loans; backers support projects to help them come to life rather than to profit financially. [5] [6]

**Operational conclusion:** If the project later runs a conventional crowdfunding campaign, treat it as reward-based funding for the pilot/season merchandise and creative deliverables. Do not include NFTs, cryptocurrencies, financial return, equity, loans, or resale-profit language in the campaign.

## U.S. Digital-Asset / Crowdfunding Context

The SEC’s April 2026 small-business resource describes a digital collectible as a crypto asset designed to be collected and/or used and potentially conveying rights to artwork, music, video, trading cards, in-game items, or references to characters/current events. It warns that a fractionalized collectible or a collectible that enables fractional ownership may be a security. The SEC’s March 2026 interpretation announcement states that the federal-law treatment of crypto assets and transactions depends on their classification and circumstances. [7] [8]

The SEC’s Regulation Crowdfunding resource describes a separate, securities-based capital-raising pathway. It requires eligible offerings to occur through an SEC-registered intermediary, contains a $5 million 12-month aggregate cap, requires disclosures, and imposes resale restrictions. [9]

FinCEN guidance states that virtual-currency users purchasing goods/services are not automatically money services businesses, but an administrator or exchanger accepting/transmitting convertible virtual currency may be a money transmitter unless an exception or limitation applies. [10]

**Operational conclusion:** Do not issue a token that offers revenue share, ownership, profit expectation, governance over funds, fractionalized character/IP rights, or a resale/appreciation narrative without specialized legal advice and a written compliance plan. Do not custody, exchange, or transmit fan crypto for others. A future digital collectible, if ever pursued, should be a consumer art/utility product with clearly described non-financial benefits and a full counsel review.
